    Somewhere in almost every lecture, the room quietly stops following. Nobody raises a hand. Each student looks around, sees no one else confused, and concludes they are the only one who did not get it, so they stay silent and the professor moves on. Psychologists call this pluralistic ignorance: most of the room privately thinks "I am lost" while assuming everyone else understood. Miller and McFarland demonstrated it in a classroom in 1987, and Karabenick and Knapp found that the students who need help most are often the least likely to ask for it. The cost compounds, because lectures build on each other. A doubt left unasked in week 3 is a wall in week 7, and by then asking is even more embarrassing.

dout is a board, one per subject. A student types the thing they did not get, with no name, no nickname and nothing stored about them, and everyone else taps **Me too** if they were stuck at the same spot. The count does two jobs at once. For the student, the tile darkens as the room agrees, and the feeling of being the only one goes with it. For the professor, it is a ranked map of where the lecture lost people. Instead of asking "any questions?" and getting silence, the next class opens on the three doubts the most students shared. It reaches the back of a 200-seat hall, the student whose first language is not English, and the one who is sure their question is stupid. None of them would have raised a hand.
